Project Overview

What This Project Is

A self-directed data analytics case study: I took a synthetic supply-chain dataset, scoped the analytical problem, built a working demand-forecasting and inventory-planning product from it, and shipped it as a live, published dashboard.

Business context

TerraTrac Equipment Parts is a fictional distributor of spare parts for heavy construction equipment (hydraulics, engine & powertrain, undercarriage, electrical/cabin, ground-engaging tools). It operates four regional distribution centers and sources from five category-specialist suppliers with different lead times and reliability. All company names and figures in the dataset are synthetic, built for this exercise.

Problem statement

TerraTrac's planning process had no formal reorder-point discipline. Nobody could answer, with evidence: which SKU/warehouse positions are actually at stockout risk right now, which suppliers' reliability claims hold up against real delivery data, and whether current demand forecasts are usable or just noise.

Project objective

Build a 12-week demand forecast, a safety-stock and reorder-point plan, and a supplier-risk assessment — grounded in the actual purchase-order history rather than stated supplier claims — and present it as a tool a supply chain leadership team could actually use.

Key questions the analysis answers

• Which SKU × warehouse positions are below their reorder point today, and how urgently?
• How accurate is a trend + seasonality + promotion forecast, measured honestly on a blind holdout?

• Which supplier carries the most lead-time risk, and does the data support the supplier master's own reliability claims?
• What should be reordered this cycle, respecting MOQ and pack size?

Expected outcome

A working, interactive dashboard covering forecast accuracy, inventory risk flags, a replenishment plan, and a supplier scorecard — published and publicly reviewable on GitHub.

Business Problem & Objectives

Why This Problem Matters

Heavy-equipment parts distribution runs on thin margins for error: a stocked-out hydraulic seal or undercarriage part can idle a customer's construction equipment, and lead times of 2–5 weeks mean a planning mistake isn't quickly correctable. Two failure modes are common in under-instrumented supply operations, and both show up in this dataset:

Silent under-stocking

Inventory drifts below what's needed to cover a lead time, and nobody notices until a stockout actually happens, because there's no reorder-point discipline flagging it in advance.

Unverified supplier claims

Reliability figures used for planning come from supplier scorecards that are rarely checked against what the purchase-order data actually shows.

Objectives

1. Produce a defensible 12-week demand forecast per SKU, correctly handling known data issues (promotions, a stockout-censored demand period) rather than ignoring them.
2. Convert that forecast into a safety-stock and reorder-point plan with a stated service-level assumption.
3. Flag stockout-risk and overstock positions, and produce a replenishment recommendation that respects MOQ and pack size.
4. Score supplier lead-time and reliability risk using actual purchase-order history, not just the stated figures.

Business questions this analysis answers

Which positions need action this week?Supply Planning tab — reorder point, safety stock, and a risk flag per SKU × warehouse.
Can we trust the forecast?Reported honestly against a naive baseline, on an out-of-sample holdout the model never saw.
Which supplier is the real risk?Supplier Risk tab — lead time and on-time rate computed from 220 actual purchase orders.

Expected analytical outcome

A forecast that measurably beats a naive baseline; a reorder-point plan grounded in a stated, defensible service-level assumption; and a supplier risk ranking backed by transaction-level evidence rather than a vendor's own scorecard.

Data Cleaning & Preprocessing

Handling the Censored-Demand Problem

The dataset's central data-quality challenge: a real 6-week supply disruption suppressed observed demand for Undercarriage SKUs at the two Asia-Pacific DCs. Reported sell-through in that window understates true demand — it's censored, not soft.

The three options, and why I picked one

OptionProblem with it
Ignore itForecast and safety stock both come out too low for the SKUs and warehouses that need the buffer most.
Exclude those weeks entirelyShrinks the training sample and biases the seasonal estimate for that calendar period.
Correct it (chosen)Reconstructs a defensible estimate of true demand without discarding data.

Method: sibling-warehouse ratio correction

For each affected SKU, I computed the average ratio of that Asia-Pacific DC's demand to the combined demand at the two unaffected DCs (Dallas, Rotterdam), using only clean, non-promotional, non-censored weeks as the basis. That ratio was then applied to the unaffected DCs' demand during the censored window to estimate what the affected DC would have sold without the disruption.

Result: measured Undercarriage demand during the censored weeks was suppressed by an average of 163% relative to the corrected estimate. Left uncorrected, this would have understated both the forecast and the safety-stock requirement for the highest-risk product category in the portfolio.

Other preprocessing steps

Promotion handling: 10 known promotional weeks cause genuine demand spikes. Rather than let them distort the trend/seasonal fit, I excluded them from the baseline fitting step and estimated a separate promotion-uplift multiplier from those weeks specifically.

Data validation: cross-checked corrected demand totals, the stockout-flag date range, and supplier reliability figures against the raw source tables rather than trusting intermediate output at face value — this is what surfaced the two calculation bugs described on page 9.

No missing-value imputation or deduplication was required — the source tables were structurally complete; the data-quality issues here are about correctness of interpretation (censored demand, unverified supplier claims), not incomplete records.

Method

Trend × seasonality × promotion-uplift regression, fit per SKU × warehouse on non-promotional weeks. Chosen over ARIMA/ETS given the data: 104 weeks is barely two full seasonal cycles, and each SKU's noise profile differs enough that a transparent, auditable model is more defensible to a planning audience than a marginally-tunable black box.

Analytical Work

Core Calculations

Two representative pieces of the analysis pipeline — not every line, just what demonstrates the actual analytical logic.

Safety stock & reorder point

Accounts for both demand variability and supplier lead-time variability — not just demand variability alone, which is the more common (and less complete) version of this formula:

safety_stock = Z * sqrt( lead_time_weeks * demand_std**2 + avg_weekly_demand**2 * lead_time_std_weeks**2 ) reorder_point = avg_weekly_demand * lead_time_weeks + safety_stock # Z = 2.05 (~98% service) for ABC Class A, 1.65 (~95%) for B/C # lead_time_weeks / lead_time_std come from actual PO history, not the supplier's stated average

Stockout-censoring correction

The sibling-warehouse ratio method described on page 6, as implemented:

# true censored weeks, from the Stockout_Flag column directly censored_weeks = demand_history.loc[demand_history.Stockout_Flag == 1, 'Week_Start_Date'].unique() sibling_avg = (demand_NA1 + demand_EU1) / 2 clean_weeks = ~(is_censored | is_promotion) ratio = (demand_AP[clean_weeks] / sibling_avg[clean_weeks]).mean() corrected_demand = ratio * sibling_avg[censored_weeks]

Why this matters more than the code itself

The formula and the correction method are both defensible on their own. What made this a real analytical exercise wasn't writing this code — it was verifying that the actual pipeline (AI-drafted from a specification) implemented them correctly. It didn't, in two places. That's the subject of the next page.

Analytical Work — Verification & Debugging

Two Bugs Found by Checking the Output Against the Data

This is the part of the project that actually demonstrates analytical judgment: not writing code, but not trusting it either — checking derived numbers against source data and catching what didn't add up.

Bug 1 — four suppliers with an "identical" 70% on-time rate

The supplier scorecard showed Trackline, Circuit & Cab, Vantage, and Ironclad all at exactly 70% on-time delivery, with Meridian at 60%. Four independently-measured suppliers landing on the exact same figure is not a real-world coincidence — it's a rounding artifact. Traced to this:

− sup_actual = po.groupby("Supplier_ID").agg( − OnTime_Pct_Actual=("On_Time_Flag", "mean"), − ).round(1) ## rounds the 0-1 FRACTION to 1 decimal first − sup_actual["OnTime_Pct_Actual"] *= 100 ## then multiplies -- precision already lost
+ sup_actual = po.groupby("Supplier_ID").agg( + OnTime_Pct_Actual=("On_Time_Flag", "mean"), + ) ## no premature rounding + sup_actual["OnTime_Pct_Actual"] = (sup_actual["OnTime_Pct_Actual"] * 100).round(1) ## round AFTER scaling
Rounding a 0–1 fraction to one decimal place before multiplying by 100 collapses any value into a 10-point bucket — 0.583, 0.651, 0.660, 0.683 all round to 0.7. Fixing the order of operations recovered the real, distinct figures: 65.1%, 58.3%, 66.0%, 68.3%, 73.7%.

Bug 2 — an off-by-one week range in the stockout correction

The censored-week correction (page 6) used a hardcoded date range instead of reading it from the data's own Stockout_Flag column:

− CENSORED_WEEKS = range(40, 46) ## weeks 40-45 -- one week early
+ CENSORED_WEEKS = range(41, 47) ## weeks 41-46 -- matches Stockout_Flag == 1
This silently corrected one week that was never actually censored, and missed the real sixth censored week entirely — wrong on both ends of the range.

What this demonstrates

Both bugs were caught the same way: by treating a generated number as a claim to verify, not a fact to accept — cross-checking supplier figures against each other (four identical values is implausible) and checking a date range against the column that actually defines it, rather than a hardcoded assumption.

Key Insights

What the Analysis Found

INSIGHT 01

26 of 28 monitored SKU × warehouse positions are below their reorder point

16 of those cannot even cover a single replenishment lead time from on-hand stock alone. This isn't concentrated in one or two problem SKUs — it's network-wide, pointing to a missing reorder-point process rather than isolated bad luck.

INSIGHT 02

Trackline Manufacturing is a structural risk, not just a slow supplier

33.4-day average actual lead time with a 10-day standard deviation — more than 3× the variability of the most consistent supplier — and the sole source for the Undercarriage category. It also caused the demand-censoring event that had to be corrected in the forecast.

INSIGHT 03

Every supplier's real on-time rate is materially worse than its stated reliability figure

A gap of 12–33 percentage points across all five suppliers, verified against 220 actual purchase orders. This isn't a Trackline-specific issue — it suggests the stated reliability field shouldn't be trusted for planning without independent verification, for any supplier.

INSIGHT 04

A seasonality + promotion-aware forecast beats a naive baseline, honestly measured

23.1% WMAPE vs. 24.0% for a 52-week moving average, on an 8-week blind holdout. The margin is real but not dramatic — reported as such rather than overstated.

INSIGHT 05

Correcting censored demand changed the picture for the highest-risk category

The Undercarriage stockout-censoring correction raised measured demand during the affected weeks by 163%. Left uncorrected, both the forecast and the safety-stock buffer for the category with the least reliable supplier would have been understated — compounding the risk instead of catching it.

Project Outcome

What Was Actually Achieved

I scoped a supply-chain analytics problem, directed AI tools to build both the analysis pipeline (Python) and the product (an interactive dashboard via Lovable), and then did the part that's easy to skip: treated that output as a first draft to verify, not a finished answer to publish as-is.

That verification step is where the actual work happened. Cross-checking the supplier scorecard surfaced four suppliers reporting an identical, statistically implausible 70% on-time rate. Tracing it led to a one-line rounding-order bug. Checking the stockout-correction logic against the raw Stockout_Flag column found a second bug — a hardcoded date range one week off from what the data actually showed. Both were fixed, the analysis was re-run, the dashboard was rebuilt from the corrected output, and the fix was verified live on the published site before this document was written.

Every number in this document, and on the live dashboard, reflects the corrected analysis — not the first version the pipeline produced.

What the analysis revealed

A network running with no functional reorder-point discipline (26 of 28 positions below target), a single-sourced supplier carrying disproportionate lead-time risk, and a supplier reliability field that couldn't be trusted at face value for any vendor in the portfolio — not just the obviously slow one.

How this demonstrates analyst skill

Not by writing the most code by hand. By not accepting generated output at face value, verifying it against source data, finding where it was wrong, and being able to explain exactly why — then communicating the corrected findings clearly enough for a non-technical stakeholder to act on.

Final summary

A working, published data product with a documented, honest accuracy claim, a defensible inventory-planning methodology, and two concretely-identified and fixed analytical bugs — the kind of scrutiny that matters more in a real analytics job than any single chart.
